# How to use the scale ruler

## In 10 seconds

1. Slide the ruler until the **KEY window** shows the key you want — e.g. `G / Em`.
2. Done. Every open hole is now a note of the scale. Every covered hole is not.

There is no step 3. That is the whole piece.

## What each hole means

| Hole | What it is | In G |
|---|---|---|
| **square** | major tonic | G |
| **diamond** | relative minor tonic | E |
| **circle** | pentatonic note | G A B D E |
| **triangle** | the full scale only (degrees 4 and 7) | C and F# |

Which gives you the most useful rule of the piece:

> **Ignore the triangles → you are playing the pentatonic.
> Use every hole → the full diatonic scale.**

It is the same hand position. The pentatonic is not a different scale: it is the diatonic
minus its two most delicate notes.

## Major or minor: only where you rest changes

The holes are **the same**. G major and E minor use the identical seven notes — what
changes is which note sounds like "home".

- A phrase ending on the **square** (G) → sounds major.
- A phrase ending on the **diamond** (E) → sounds minor.

Try it on the neck with the ruler parked in the same place: play the same sequence of
holes twice, once ending on the square and once on the diamond. The character flips
inside out and you did not change a single note.

## The 5 shapes

The table on top splits the ruler into **5 boxes**. Each box is one position (the CAGED
boxes). Where the box starts, the shape starts; where it ends, it ends.

- **Top** number = the shape's number thinking in the **major key**.
- **Bottom** number = the same shape thinking in the **relative minor**.

The vertical lines fall on frets that belong to **both neighboring shapes** — that is why
the shapes join up instead of sitting isolated. That shared fret is your bridge for
changing position without stopping the phrase.

On the 6th string there are **two squares**, 12 frets apart: from one to the other is a
whole octave. The 5 shapes fit exactly in that stretch — after that everything repeats.

### The ruler shows one octave of the MAJOR key

That has a consequence that trips you up the first time. In `C / Am`, the ruler covers
frets **8 to 20** — one octave counted from the C. The minor tonic (A) sits 3 frets
*before* the C, so **shape 1 of Am**, the one you are used to playing on **fret 5**,
shows up on the ruler over at the right end, on **fret 17**.

It is not another shape: 17 − 12 = 5. It is the same box one octave up, note for note.

That is why the fret window shows **two numbers**: the big one is the fret you are on,
the small one is its twin one octave along. Saw `17` with a `5` underneath? You can play
that same shape on 5 and get the same thing — only the region of the neck changes.

## Practice routine

**1 — One shape at a time.**
Put `G / Em` in the window. Play only the holes inside **box 1**, from the lowest string
to the highest and back. Finish on the square. Then box 2, box 3… up to 5.

**2 — Pentatonic only.**
Same thing, ignoring the triangles. Compare: it is the same shape with two gaps.

**3 — Joining boxes.**
Go up through box 1 and, when you reach the fret with the line, carry on into box 2
without stopping. Then come back. That shared fret is the only thing you need to memorize
for the whole neck to become one single thing.

**4 — Changing the center.**
Without touching the ruler, play a phrase resolving on the square and the same one
resolving on the diamond.

**5 — Changing key.**
Slide one fret: `G/Em` becomes `G#/Fm`. **The drawing did not change.** Play the same
shape in the new place. That is the reason the ruler has uniform spacing instead of
imitating a real neck — the pattern holds in all 12 keys.

## The two small notes

In G, the triangles are **C** (degree 4) and **F#** (degree 7). They are the seasoning:

- **F#** pulls towards the G. A great note to end a run on before landing on the tonic.
- **C** fights the G major chord if you hold it — it works well in passing, badly as a
  long note.

That is exactly why the pentatonic exists: take those two out and any note sounds fine
over any chord in the field. Start with the big holes and add the small ones when your
ear asks for them.

## Studying one shape at a time — the cassette

The cassette runs in a channel **below** the slider, between it and the paper. It is two
masks joined together with a **4-fret window** between them. Where a mask sits, the slider
holes show its blank face instead of the note; where the window sits, you see the notes.

Slide it until the window lands on the box you want: the rest of the neck goes dark and
only that shape is left. One move. It is the most honest way to study a position — you
cannot "escape" into the neighbouring box without noticing.

Shapes 3 and 5 are 4 frets wide and fill the window exactly. Shapes 1, 2 and 4 are 3 frets
wide, and for those you bring in the **blade**: one fret wide, running in its own channel,
it closes the column left over. On the wide shapes you park it out at the end of the track.

The blade ends up under the slider, so you work it by its **tails** — the thin rails that
stick out past the slider at the near edge. **Pull the tail on the side you want the blade
to move towards; never push.** One of the two is always reachable.

The shape table, the KEY window and the fret numbers stay visible the whole time, through
the openings above and below the mask band. Those same openings are where you put your
finger to move the cassette.

To get the whole neck back, slide the cassette out to either end.

## Together with the harmonic field wheel

Turn the wheel until `G` shows up in the middle window. It gives you the 7 chords of the
key. Put `G / Em` on the ruler: it gives you the notes to play over any of them.

Same key, two halves of the same problem — chords on one piece, notes on the other.
